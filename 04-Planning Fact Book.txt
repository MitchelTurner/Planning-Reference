LEGAL FOOTING
How to use this

Every decision rests on two things: the adopted Borough code and the 2035 Comprehensive Plan. Strong Towns and Confessions of a Recovering Engineer explain why those rules make sense, and they help you write better rules. They are not the legal test for a permit.

The legal footing. Under AS 29.40.040, the Assembly adopts land use regulations to carry out the comprehensive plan. The plan says the same: second-class boroughs must adopt a plan and implement it through zoning Exec Sum p. 2. The statute also bars variances for conditions the applicant created, for prohibited uses, or for financial hardship alone.

THE FRAMEWORK
Strong Towns: the core ideas and the evidence

Spread-out growth produces tax revenue now but leaves long-term maintenance costs the tax base can’t cover. Compact, incremental growth on existing streets and pipes pays its way.

IDEA	WHAT IT SAYS	EVIDENCE
The growth Ponzi scheme	New roads and pipes bring revenue at first. Their replacement costs arrive decades later and exceed what the development pays in tax.	Lafayette, LA: $32B in infrastructure replacement liability against a $16B tax base. Maintenance alone would take about $3,300 a year from a median household paying about $1,500 in local tax. Strong Towns, 2017
Value per acre	Judge development by tax value per acre, not per project. Land and infrastructure are the scarce, costly inputs.	Asheville, NC: a Super Walmart paid about $6,500/acre in property tax. A downtown mixed-use building with 19 condos paid about $634,000/acre. Urban3, 2016
Next increment by right	Every neighborhood can take the next small step without special permission: a duplex, an ADU, a corner store.	“No neighborhood should experience radical change, but no neighborhood can be exempt from change.” Strong Towns
Parking minimums	Required parking eats land and adds cost. Let builders and lenders decide how much to provide.	After Buffalo dropped minimums, 36 major projects built 21% fewer spaces than required. Mixed-use projects built 53% fewer. Hess & Rehler, JAPA 2021
Small builders	Many small local builders make a resilient housing market. Rules sized for large developers shut them out.	In the plan: pre-approved small-house plans Housing 2a and a developer roundtable on infill barriers Housing 1g

Growth that can’t pay for its own replacement needs more growth

After Strong Towns · 4 stages, one loop

Why it fits Ketchikan. The Borough owns 0.5% of the land, the population is forecast to fall, and service-area residents pay for road maintenance. Building inward on sewered, paved, plowed streets is the cheapest way to add housing. It is also the only way that doesn’t grow the per-household maintenance bill.

The rebuild rule
A nonconforming building damaged by accident can be rebuilt with a permit within a year. One torn down from ordinary wear “must be rebuilt in accordance with the development standards of the zone” (KGBC 18.30.030). An old house inside today’s setbacks can’t be replaced in its own footprint without a variance. That likely explains much of the dozens of setback variances heard each year.

Commercial parking adds up. A restaurant needs 1 space per 5 seats plus half a space per employee. Retail needs 1 per 250 sq ft of display area plus half per employee KGBC 18.70.060. Few historic storefronts could supply that on site.

The test to run. Ask staff for a GIS count of RM and RH parcels under 5,000 sq ft or 50 ft wide, and of buildings inside current setbacks. If a large share of the older town is nonconforming, that is the local case for smaller lots and a Juneau-style alternative standard.

What it means in Ketchikan
Downtown and Tongass Avenue are streets, not roads. They carry storefronts, crosswalks, and more than 1.5 million visitors each season. Transportation 5b already calls for better crossings, sidewalks, and protected bike lanes. Most of the corridor is state-managed (road jurisdiction map, Appx A p. 11), so the Borough’s tool is advocacy with DOT&PF and the STIP.
North and South Tongass Highway are roads. Keep them fast and safe. Limit new driveways and strip commercial. Group development into nodes like Ward Cove and Herring Cove.
Neighborhood streets should be narrow. Narrow streets cost less to build, plow, and repave. The infrastructure working group raised flexible road standards (Appx C p. 123). That is a Title 17 change.
Cruise-season congestion is a reason to slow downtown, not widen it. There is no room to widen along the viaduct, and added capacity fills back up. Coach staggering Transportation 5d manages demand instead.

Q&A
Answers to common challenges

Lead with the Ketchikan fact or the plan citation, then the outside evidence. In a hearing, use only what is in the record and the code criteria.

Expand all
Collapse all
“More housing will mean more traffic.”

Summer congestion comes from about 1.5 million cruise visitors on a town of 13,500. A few dozen new homes don’t cause it. Homes near downtown make shorter trips, and many can be walked.

Widening doesn’t fix congestion: driving grows in proportion to added lane miles (Duranton & Turner 2011). The plan manages demand instead Transportation 5d.
“Where will everyone park?”

The builder and the lender still decide how much parking a project needs. Ending the minimum only stops the code from forcing more than the market wants.

In Buffalo, projects still built parking, just 21% less than the old code required (Hess & Rehler 2021). Anchorage dropped minimums citywide in 2022. The plan supports relaxed parking for workforce housing Housing 2c.
“Apartments will hurt the neighborhood.”

The adopted Future Land Use Map already lists fourplexes and townhomes for Urban Residential Exec Sum p. 8. The Strong Towns rule: no neighborhood sees radical change, and none is exempt from change.

Research mostly measures rents, not values: new 50+ unit buildings in lower-income areas cut rents within about two blocks by 5–7% (Upjohn Institute, 2020).
“Why not just open up more land out the road?”

The Borough owns 0.5% of the land, population is projected to fall about 20% by 2050, and service-area residents pay for road upkeep. Every new mile of road is a permanent bill for fewer people.

Lafayette, LA ended up with twice as much infrastructure liability as tax base (Strong Towns). The plan requires feasibility studies before deciding the future of large undeveloped tracts Appx A p. 3.
“It will change the character of the neighborhood.”

Character is mostly about building size, setbacks, and how buildings meet the street. A duplex can look like a house.

The plan supports design standards that protect character while allowing housing Land Use 2b. Juneau’s downtown overlay was created to protect its historic pattern.
“People said they want moderate growth.”

They did, at 42% Exec Sum p. 4. Small infill on existing streets is moderate growth.

The same survey ranked affordable housing first, at 71% Appx C p. 82.
“Slower streets will hurt business or slow emergency response.”

Downtown business runs on foot traffic. Emergency access is a real constraint: set narrow-street standards with the Fire Department, not around it.

A pedestrian hit at 42 mph has a 50% risk of dying, against 10% at 23 mph (Tefft 2011).
“Tongass Avenue is a State road. Why is this our business?”

The plan directs the Borough to push for safer crossings and sidewalks there Transportation 5b.

The 2024–2027 STIP lists more than $50 million in regional projects and “emphasizes that local advocacy is essential” (Core Plan, Transportation).
“Government shouldn’t be in the housing business.”

Agreed, and most of these changes remove rules rather than spend money. Larger lots, required parking, and hearings for routine setbacks are government requirements.

The housing working group itself noted little appetite for government investment in housing (Appx C p. 134).
CREDIBILITY
Honest limits

Strong Towns is a framework, not settled science. Naming where it strains in Ketchikan before a critic does makes the rest of the case more credible.

LIMIT	WHY IT MATTERS HERE	HOW TO HANDLE IT
Value per acre is a simplification	It counts revenue, not the cost of serving each acre. Sales tax is mostly tourism, so property value alone undercounts commercial land.	Show property and sales tax together. Pair revenue with service and replacement costs.
Steep terrain and landslides	Infill on steep lots costs more and can raise hazard risk.	Hazards come before density. Use the Environmental Constraints overlay Appx A p. 19.
Fire and emergency access	The International Fire Code generally calls for 20 ft of clear width on fire access roads (confirm locally).	Write narrow-street standards with the Fire Department. Use parking limits and turnouts, not just narrower pavement.
The State controls Tongass Avenue	DOT&PF sets its design standards and budget.	Advocate through the STIP and Transportation 5b. Focus Borough effort on City and service-area streets.
Allowing infill doesn’t guarantee it	Building costs are high and the market is small. The working group heard there are few attractive lots available in the City.	Pair code changes with the land inventory, pre-approved plans, and the developer roundtable Housing 1c 2a 1g.
Sewer limits density	Urban Residential density depends on sewer, and the record conflicts on capacity.	Get a written capacity answer before upzoning Public Facilities 2.
Population is projected to fall	Upzoning may produce less building if demand drops.	The shortage is current, and the 16% vacancy figure includes seasonal and short-term rentals. Track permits Housing 1b.
Parking in a rainy, hilly town	Walking and transit have limits in winter; downtown parking is scarce in summer.	Start with downtown and workforce housing Housing 2c, then measure before going further.
The speed data is a national average	Tefft’s curve is for an average pedestrian. Older pedestrians face higher risk at every speed.	This cuts in favor of slower streets. Say so.
